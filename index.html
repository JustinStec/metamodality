All texts provided by the organizers. Participants from all disciplinary backgrounds welcome.

Unit I: Ancient and Medieval Foundations

Week 1 — February 4: Parmenides (~40 pp.)

How can we think or speak "what is not"? This problem drives subsequent responses from Aristotle through Heidegger. Kingsley's reading situates Parmenides within an initiatory tradition.

  • Parmenides, "On Nature" (complete fragments, ~10 pp.)
  • Peter Kingsley, In the Dark Places of Wisdom, chapters 1–4 (~30 pp.)

Week 2 — February 11: Aristotle (~45 pp.)

Aristotle's potentiality (dynamis) answers Parmenides by locating possibility within beings themselves. Avicenna and Heidegger will both transform this vocabulary. The sea-battle problem asks whether future contingents have determinate truth values.

  • Aristotle, Metaphysics Book Θ, chapters 1–3 and 6–8 (~30 pp.)
  • Aristotle, De Interpretatione, chapter 9 (~5 pp.)
  • Charlotte Witt, "The Priority of Actuality in Aristotle" (~10 pp.)

Week 3 — February 18: Avicenna (~40 pp.)

Separating essence from existence, Avicenna makes contingency fundamental to everything except the Necessary Existent. This reconfiguration of Aristotle shapes Leibniz's later work on possible worlds and sufficient reason.

  • Avicenna, The Metaphysics of The Healing, Book I chapters 5–6, Book VIII chapters 1–3 (~25 pp.)
  • Peter Adamson, "From the Necessary Existent to God" (~15 pp.)

Week 4 — February 25: Nagarjuna (~40 pp.)

Does "possibility" mean anything once inherent existence drops out? Nagarjuna's tetralemma refuses the logical options that Western frameworks take for granted, and the contemplative science readings in Unit IV will return to this question.

  • Nagarjuna, Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, chapters 1, 15, 24 (Garfield trans., ~20 pp.)
  • Jay Garfield, "Dependent Arising and the Emptiness of Emptiness" (~20 pp.)

Unit I Workshop — February 26 or 27

Unit II: Rationalism, Transcendentalism, and the Imaginal

Week 5 — March 4: Leibniz (~40 pp.)

Compossibility constrains which possibilities can coexist within a single world. Leibniz's framework, which Kripke and Lewis later formalize, raises the question of what individuates possible worlds and whether they exist independently of minds.

  • Leibniz, Monadology (~15 pp.)
  • Leibniz, Discourse on Metaphysics, sections 1–14 (~20 pp.)
  • Leibniz, "On the Ultimate Origination of Things" (~5 pp., selections)

Week 6 — March 11: Kant (~40 pp.)

What must any possible experience conform to? Kant's Postulates distinguish logical possibility from real possibility, a distinction Derrida will later destabilize.

  • Kant, Critique of Pure Reason: "Postulates of Empirical Thought" (A218–235/B265–287, ~20 pp.)
  • Kant, Critique of Pure Reason: Introduction (B1–18, ~20 pp.)

Week 7 — March 18: Ibn Arabi (~35 pp.) (Spring Break—informal)

Ibn Arabi's imaginal world (ʿālam al-mithāl) occupies a realm between sensible and intelligible, where possibilities take on form. His framework transforms Avicenna's essence/existence distinction and anticipates Leibniz's concern with why these possibilities rather than others become actual.

  • Ibn Arabi, Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam (Bezels of Wisdom), "Adam" chapter (~10 pp., Austin or Dagli trans.)
  • William Chittick, The Sufi Path of Knowledge, chapter 1 (~25 pp.)

Unit II Workshop — March 19 or 20

Unit III: Phenomenology, Pragmatism, and Nothingness

Week 8 — March 25: Husserl and Derrida (~45 pp.)

Can a condition of possibility also be a condition of impossibility? Derrida finds this structure in Husserl's account of ideal objects, which arise in history yet claim eternal validity.

  • Husserl, "The Origin of Geometry" (~20 pp.)
  • Derrida, "Différance" (from Margins of Philosophy, ~25 pp.)

Week 9 — April 1: Heidegger (~45 pp.)

Heidegger transforms the Aristotelian vocabulary so that possibility becomes constitutive of Dasein. Death individualizes as the ownmost possibility, and the analysis connects to Aristotle's priority question about potentiality and actuality.

  • Heidegger, Being and Time, Division II chapter 1 (§§46–53, ~35 pp.)
  • Heidegger, Basic Problems of Phenomenology, §11a (~10 pp.)

Week 10 — April 8: Peirce (~40 pp.)

Firstness is pure qualitative possibility, prior to relation or resistance. Peirce's continuum allows possibility to grade into actuality, and his categories resurface in Whitehead's process philosophy.

  • Peirce, "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (~20 pp.)
  • Peirce, "A Guess at the Riddle", chapters 1–3 (~20 pp.)

Week 11 — April 15: Nishida (~45 pp.)

Nishida's "place of absolute nothingness" (zettai mu no basho) holds particulars without being one itself. His synthesis of Zen Buddhism and Western phenomenology offers a different response to the Parmenidean problem than Aristotle's, and connects to Nagarjuna's emptiness while engaging directly with Heidegger.

  • Nishida Kitarō, An Inquiry into the Good, Part I chapters 1–3 (~25 pp.)
  • Nishida Kitarō, "The Logic of Topos and the Religious Worldview" (~20 pp.)

Unit III Workshop — April 17 or 18

Unit IV: Process, Contemplation, Emanation

Week 12 — April 22: Whitehead (~45 pp.)

Eternal objects ingress into actual occasions as pure potentials. Whitehead's God functions as the primordial envisagement of possibility, a role that recalls Leibniz, and Peirce's categories find new form in this processual framework.

  • Whitehead, Science and the Modern World, chapter 11 (~25 pp.)
  • Whitehead, Process and Reality, Part I section 2.1 (~20 pp.)

Week 13 — April 29: Contemplative Science (~45 pp.)

Thompson and Varela bridge first-person contemplative methods and third-person neuroscience. The Buddhist concept of awareness (rigpa) may constitute a kind of possibility prior to Western categories, returning to questions Nagarjuna raised.

  • Evan Thompson, Waking, Dreaming, Being, chapter 1 (~25 pp.)
  • Francisco Varela, "Neurophenomenology: A Methodological Remedy for the Hard Problem" (~20 pp.)

Week 14 — May 6: Neoplatonism (~40 pp.)

Possibility flows from the One's inexhaustible superabundance in Plotinus and Proclus. This emanationist framework shaped Avicenna, passed into German Idealism, and offers a counterpoint to the Aristotelian model of potentiality.

  • Plotinus, Enneads V.1 (~25 pp.)
  • Proclus, Elements of Theology, Propositions 1–25 (~15 pp.)

Unit IV Workshop — May 7 or 8

Unit V: Mathematical and Formal Possibility

Week 15 — May 13: Hamkins (~45 pp.)

Many distinct concepts of set exist in Hamkins's multiverse, each instantiated in a corresponding universe. Potentialism treats the hierarchy as indefinitely extensible, echoing Aristotle's claim that actuality precedes potentiality while reversing its valence.

  • Hamkins, "The Set-Theoretic Multiverse" (2012, ~25 pp.)
  • Hamkins and Linnebo, "The Modal Logic of Set-Theoretic Potentialism" (2022, ~20 pp.)

Week 16 — May 20: Foster (~40 pp.)

How do possibility spaces themselves get produced? Foster's work shows tradition constraining what becomes thinkable while furnishing materials for innovation, bringing the semester's philosophical questions into contact with computational and sociological methods.

  • Foster, "Culture and Computation" (Poetics, 2018, ~15 pp.)
  • Foster, Rzhetsky, and Evans, "Tradition and Innovation in Scientists' Research Strategies" (ASR, 2015, ~25 pp.)
